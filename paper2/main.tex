\begin{abstract}
Blockage-aware proactive handover in millimeter-wave (mmWave) networks
switches a user to another cell before a body crosses the line of sight
(LoS) to its radio unit. Predicting such a crossing requires knowing
where the user is, so the scheme is only as good as the network's
positioning. This paper asks whether the radio units that serve a user
can localize it accurately enough from its uplink sounding reference
signals. We derive single-epoch position error bounds that treat
synchronization and array calibration errors as Bayesian priors,
compare LoS-only information with map-aided information from a digital
twin, and evaluate a dominant-path estimator and a map-aided multipath
tracker. Ray-traced experiments in a street canyon, reported on
held-out scenarios, show that array calibration, not bandwidth, limits
the bound, and that blockage substantially degrades the LoS-only bound,
whereas known reflected paths keep the map-aided bound much lower. The
dominant-path estimator has mostly systematic errors that grow sharply
around blockage events, and its positions make proactive handover worse
than 3GPP event-triggered handover. The map-aided tracker reduces the
median error from \pEstMedFourHundred{} to \pTrkMed\,cm and keeps it low
around blockage onsets; with its positions, proactive handover is no
longer significantly worse than the 3GPP event at margins of
\SI{15}{dB} and above, but not better either.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
Millimeter-wave (mmWave) bands provide the bandwidth that 5G and 6G
networks need for high data rates, but their links are fragile: a
pedestrian or vehicle crossing the line of sight (LoS) attenuates the
signal by tens of decibels within milliseconds
\cite{rappaport2013mmwave,maccartney2017blockage}. Reactive handover
cannot avoid such outages. Integrated sensing and communication (ISAC) and
other sensing-aided approaches therefore aim to predict blockage and
hand the user over in advance
\cite{liu2022isac,charan2021vision,li2025lookbefore}.

Predicting a blockage means predicting whether a body will cross the
line between a radio unit and the user. The prediction can thus be no
better than the network's knowledge of where the user is. In a companion
study \cite{natanzi2027companion}, we found that a blockage-aware
handover planner outperforms the service-aware 3GPP A5 event only if
the user position is known with an error of about \pBreakEven\,cm (per
horizontal axis), and that a meter-level error removes the benefit
entirely.

Can the network meet this requirement with the infrastructure it
already has? 5G NR supports uplink positioning from sounding reference
signals (SRS) \cite{3gpp38855}, and recent street-scale studies with
several base stations report errors below a meter
\cite{dong2026deployable}. Position error bounds for mmWave systems
suggest that much better accuracy is possible in principle
\cite{shahmansoori2018position,abushaban2018bounds}, but they typically
consider ideal hardware or a single impairment in static scenes
\cite{keskin2022optimal}. It remains unclear which effect
limits decimeter accuracy in practice, how blockage changes it, how
close practical estimators come, and whether their errors are small
enough when handover decisions are made.

This paper addresses these questions in a street canyon served by two
O-RAN radio units (O-RUs). Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item A positioning analysis for blockage-aware handover: a
single-epoch equivalent Fisher information analysis with
synchronization and array calibration errors as Bayesian priors, two
models of a blocked LoS, and map-aided information from reflected paths
with known geometry.
\item Two practical uplink estimators: a dominant-path estimator with an
extended Kalman filter (EKF), whose single-epoch front end is compared
with the bound by decomposing its error into bias and random spread, and
a map-aided multipath tracker that associates extracted paths with
virtual anchors of the digital twin.
\item An analysis of positioning errors around blockage onsets and a
closing experiment that feeds estimated and synthetic bound-level
positions into a blockage-aware handover planner. The bounds, the
estimator, and the scripts that generate every number in this paper are
released as open source.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CLIS-WPI/sense2connect}}
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\textit{Bounds for mmWave positioning.} Fisher information analyses
show that the angle and delay resolution of mmWave arrays support
sub-decimeter accuracy \cite{shahmansoori2018position,abushaban2018bounds},
building on the equivalent Fisher information framework for wideband
localization \cite{shen2010fundamental}. Multipath components with known
geometry carry position information as virtual anchors
\cite{leitinger2015evaluation,witrisal2016high}, and multipath tracking
associates them with such anchors probabilistically
\cite{meyer2018message}. We add hardware errors as priors and evaluate
bounds and estimators under traffic-driven blockage.

\textit{Uplink SRS positioning.} 3GPP specifies uplink time- and
angle-based positioning \cite{3gpp38855}. Dong \emph{et al.}
\cite{dong2026deployable} combine LoS/NLoS gating, dominant-path angle
and delay estimation, and multi-base-station EKF fusion, reach sub-meter
errors in a ray-traced campus, and point to reflections as virtual
anchors for future work.

\textit{Sensing-aided handover.} Vision-, radar-, and ISAC-aided
handover predicts blockage or user motion
\cite{charan2021vision,li2025lookbefore}; our companion study
\cite{natanzi2027companion} identified the user position as a binding
requirement.

\section{System Model and Problem Formulation}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Signal Model}
A single-antenna user equipment (UE) at horizontal position $\mathbf p$
at known height (distances and directions are evaluated at the 3D point
$[\mathbf p^{\mathsf T},z_{\mathrm{UE}}]^{\mathsf T}$) transmits an uplink SRS that is received by a set $\mathcal C$ of O-RUs,
each with an $M$-element array. At O-RU $c$, subcarrier frequency
$f_n$, and element $m$, the received sample is
\begin{equation}
y_{c,n,m}=\sum_{\ell}\beta_{c,\ell}\,
e^{-j2\pi f_n(\tau_{c,\ell}+\delta_c+b)}\,
e^{j\psi_{c,m}}\,s_m(\mathbf u_{c,\ell})+w_{c,n,m},
\label{eq:signal}
\end{equation}
where path $\ell$ has complex gain $\beta_{c,\ell}$ (including blockage
loss), delay $\tau_{c,\ell}$, and arrival direction
$\mathbf u_{c,\ell}$ with steering response $s_m$; $\delta_c$ is the time
offset of O-RU $c$, $b$ the UE clock bias, $\psi_{c,m}$ the calibration
phase error of element $m$, and $w_{c,n,m}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma_w^2)$
independent thermal noise. Hardware
errors are random with $\delta_c\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{sync}}^2)$
and $\psi_{c,m}\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_\phi^2)$.

\subsection{Geometry and Blockage}
The LoS delay and angles are functions of $\mathbf p$ and the O-RU
position $\mathbf p_c$, e.g., $\tau_{c,0}=\|\mathbf p-\mathbf p_c\|/c_0$.
A specular reflection on a known surface is an LoS path from the virtual
anchor $\tilde{\mathbf p}_{c,\ell}$, the image of $\mathbf p_c$ in that
surface, so that $\tau_{c,\ell}=\|\mathbf p-\tilde{\mathbf p}_{c,\ell}\|/c_0$
\cite{leitinger2015evaluation}. Moving bodies attenuate every path
segment according to a blockage model; we use 3GPP model~B
\cite{3gpp38901}. An O-RU is \emph{blocked} for a UE when the loss of its LoS path exceeds
a threshold. Model~B reduces the power of a blocked LoS
but keeps its geometry, whereas the field that actually arrives
diffracts around the blocker. We therefore consider two models of a
blocked LoS: \emph{kept}, in which its delay and angles still follow
$\mathbf p$ (optimistic), and \emph{biased}, in which they carry an
unknown bias. In the biased model, a bound treats them as free parameters, and an
estimator observes a blocked LoS whose delay and direction deviate from
the geometry of $\mathbf p$.

\subsection{Positioning Requirement of Blockage-Aware Handover}
A blockage-aware handover policy $\pi$ predicts the SNR of each cell
from the tracked blockers and the reported UE position
$\hat{\mathbf p}_t=\mathbf p_t+\mathbf e_t$, and selects the serving cell
accordingly. Its outage time $J(\pi;\{\hat{\mathbf p}_t\})$ counts the
time below the SNR required for the service rate, including the
interruptions of its handovers. Since the policy aims to outperform a
reactive reference policy $\pi_0$ (here, the 3GPP A5 event), the
positioning requirement is that the error process $\{\mathbf e_t\}$ keeps
\begin{equation}
J\bigl(\pi;\{\hat{\mathbf p}_t\}\bigr)\le J(\pi_0).
\label{eq:req}
\end{equation}
This requirement depends on the error at the times the policy acts, not
only on its overall statistics. We therefore examine (i) what limits
the position error bound, (ii) how blockage and map knowledge change it,
(iii) how a practical estimator compares, and (iv) whether its errors
satisfy \eqref{eq:req}.

\subsection{Single-Epoch Position Error Bound}
Collect the channel parameters of all O-RUs at one epoch in
$\boldsymbol\eta$ (delays, angles, complex gains) and the hardware and
timing errors in $\boldsymbol\nu=[\{\delta_c\},b,\{\psi_{c,m}\}]$. The
Fisher information of $\boldsymbol\eta$ follows from \eqref{eq:signal}
by the Slepian--Bangs formula; we compute it exactly by factorizing its
frequency and antenna sums. With $\mathbf T=\partial\boldsymbol\eta/\partial\mathbf p$,
the equivalent Fisher information of the position is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf J_{\mathbf p}=\mathbf T^{\mathsf T}\mathbf J_{\boldsymbol\eta}\mathbf T
-\mathbf T^{\mathsf T}\mathbf J_{\boldsymbol\eta\boldsymbol\nu}
\bigl(\mathbf J_{\boldsymbol\nu\boldsymbol\nu}+\boldsymbol\Lambda\bigr)^{-1}
\mathbf J_{\boldsymbol\nu\boldsymbol\eta}\mathbf T,
\label{eq:efim}
\end{equation}
where all blocks are those remaining after the channel nuisance
parameters have been eliminated jointly by a Schur complement: the
complex gains of all paths; in the \emph{LoS-only} bound, the delays and
angles of all reflected paths; and in the biased model, the delay and
angles of a blocked LoS. The second term eliminates the timing and
hardware errors in $\boldsymbol\nu$. $\boldsymbol\Lambda$ holds the Gaussian priors of the
O-RU time offsets ($\sigma_{\mathrm{sync}}$) and of the element phases
($\sigma_\phi$); the UE clock bias has no prior (time difference of
arrival, TDoA). In the \emph{map-aided} bound, the delays and angles of the reflected
paths are not nuisance parameters: their geometry is known from the digital twin,
and their entries of $\mathbf T$ follow analytically from the virtual
anchors. The analytic derivatives agree with central finite differences
at \pDerivFdNumUe{} random UE positions to a relative error of at most
\pDerivFdMaxRel, and the GPU implementation of \eqref{eq:efim} matches
an independent NumPy implementation to \pFimJobErr{} and a brute-force
numerical Fisher matrix on toy cases to \pFimToyErr; further checks are
in the repository. The position
error bound is $\mathrm{PEB}=\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf J_{\mathbf p}^{-1})}$.
Because the hardware errors enter through priors, it is a hybrid
Cram\'er--Rao bound: it bounds the root-mean-square (RMS) error of
unbiased single-epoch estimates for a given geometry, averaged over the
noise and the hardware priors; it is neither a median error nor a
guarantee that an estimator attains it, a biased estimator can have a
smaller spread, and it excludes the temporal information that a
tracking filter uses.

\subsection{Dominant-Path Estimator}
Per O-RU and epoch, a zero-padded two-dimensional DFT over the array
selects the strongest beam; a Hann-windowed delay transform of the
beamformed signal gives the delay of the dominant path, refined by
parabolic interpolation and followed by an angle refinement at that
delay. A peak-to-second-peak test flags reflected dominant paths.
Because a planar array of isotropic elements cannot distinguish the two
sides of its plane, each angle yields two direction hypotheses; the
estimator keeps the joint hypothesis that is consistent across O-RUs,
lies in the walkable area of the digital-twin map, and passes the gate
of a constant-velocity EKF, which fuses weighted least-squares fixes
over time.

\subsection{Map-Aided Multipath Tracker}
Per O-RU and epoch, a sparse extraction with continuous refinement
returns up to eight multipath components with variances from the local Fisher information. The sources
are the LoS and every single- and double-bounce virtual anchor of the
street-facing building faces and the ground that is geometrically valid
at the predicted position. Loopy belief propagation computes the
probabilities that a source generated a component or that the component
is clutter, with constant visibility priors and a blocked mode that
models a detoured LoS by an inflated covariance \cite{meyer2018message};
a probabilistic-data-association EKF with a
constant-velocity model then updates the position, velocity, UE clock
bias, and O-RU time offsets, and projects the position onto the
walkable area. A grid search over the walkable area initializes the
track and re-initializes it after a loss of association.

\subsection{Setup}
\textit{Scenario.} We use the street canyon of
\cite{natanzi2027companion} (Fig.~\ref{fig:scenario}): buildings
\pStreetWidth\,m apart, two \pLaneWidth-m lanes, and sidewalks on both
sides; actors and UEs move on a periodic section of \pStreetPeriod\,m.
Two O-RUs with \pArray{} half-wavelength arrays at \pCarrier\,GHz form
two neighboring cells that share this section: O-RU~0 on the north side,
either on a lamppost (\pOruZeroLampZ\,m high) or on a facade
(\pOruZeroFacZ\,m), and O-RU~1 on a \pOruOneLampZ-m lamppost on the south
sidewalk, \pOruOffset\,m further along the street. Pedestrian UEs at
\pUeHeight\,m walk along the south sidewalk at \pUeSpeed\,m/s. Each
\pRunDuration-s run has low or high traffic: \pVehLaneKmLow{} or
\pVehLaneKmHigh{} vehicles per lane and km (cars, buses, trucks at
\pVehSpeed\,m/s) and \pPedWalkersLow{} or \pPedWalkersHigh{} pedestrians
on the sidewalks plus \pPedCrossingLow{} or \pPedCrossingHigh{} crossing
the street. Paths are traced with Sionna RT
\cite{hoydis2023sionnart,sionna} (specular, order up to \pMaxDepth, at
most \pMaxPaths{} paths per link).

\textit{Service model.} The SNR of a cell adds, over all traced paths,
the path power with the full array gain after its model-B loss; this is
an optimistic approximation of the received multipath power, since no
single beam collects all paths with full gain. The UE is in outage when
this total remaining power gives an SNR below
$\gamma_{\mathrm{req}}=\pSNRreq$\,dB, the value needed for
\SI{400}{Mbit/s}; during \SI{10}{dB} LoS events, the strongest
surviving path of the same cell lies a median \pSameCellLoss\,dB below
the unblocked LoS \cite{natanzi2027companion}.

\textit{Handover model.} The policy is the planner of
\cite{natanzi2027companion}: every \pEpoch\,s it receives the blocker
tracks and the reported UE position, predicts the SNR of each cell by
applying model~B on the line between each O-RU and the reported
position, and chooses the cell sequence that minimizes the predicted
outage over a horizon of \pPlanH\,s, with an interruption of \pTauHO\,ms
per handover; decisions take effect after a control-loop delay of
\pLoopDelay\,ms, and the sensing overhead of \pOverhead{} of the
resources is charged to it. The A5 thresholds and time-to-trigger are
tuned per margin on the tuning seeds; the tuned time-to-trigger,
\SI{40}{ms}, is the smallest value of the grid. With perfect blocker tracks and a white
Gaussian UE position error, it satisfies \eqref{eq:req} only up to a
per-axis standard deviation of about \pBreakEven\,cm, i.e., an RMS
distance error of about \pBreakEvenRms\,cm or a median distance error of
about \pBreakEvenMedDist\,cm \cite{natanzi2027companion}.

\textit{Blocked LoS.} An O-RU is blocked for a UE at an epoch when the
model-B loss of its LoS path is at least \pBlockedDb\,dB. In the biased
model, the signal fed to the estimator replaces the blocked LoS by a
detour around its dominant blocker (the blocker with the largest model-B
loss): with $\mathbf q$ the shorter of the detour points over the top
and past either side of the blocker box, next to the point of the LoS
closest to the blocker, the path keeps the attenuated complex gain of
the LoS but has delay
$(\|\mathbf q-\mathbf p_c\|+\|\mathbf p-\mathbf q\|)/c_0$ and arrival
direction along $\mathbf q-\mathbf p_c$; reflected paths are only
attenuated.

\textit{Signals and hardware.} The UE transmits one SRS symbol per
\pEpoch-s epoch at \pTxPower\,dBm, spread uniformly over the active
subcarriers; the O-RU noise figure is
\pNoiseFig\,dB. Numerology~\pNumerology{} (\pScs\,kHz) with \pBwA,
\pBwB, and \pBwC\,MHz carriers uses \pNscA, \pNscB, and \pNscC{} active
subcarriers \cite{3gpp38101_2}. We sweep
$\sigma_{\mathrm{sync}}$ of \pSyncListShown\,ns (drawn once per run, a
bias that the EKF cannot average out; 40 realizations over the held-out
runs) and $\sigma_\phi$ of \pPhiList\,deg
(fixed per O-RU and run). The main configuration uses TDoA, \pBwC\,MHz,
$\sigma_{\mathrm{sync}}=\pSyncMain$\,ns, $\sigma_\phi=\pPhiMain$\,deg, and
the biased blocked-LoS model.

\textit{Protocol.} Estimator parameters were tuned on seeds
\pSeedsTune{} and methods fixed on development seeds \pSeedsDev. An
earlier held-out set was consumed before the front/back disambiguation
was added, so all results come from a second, never-used set of ten
seeds (\pSeedsHeld), each simulated for two mounts and two traffic
densities. The seed is the statistical unit: we average its runs,
compare conditions with the exact two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test
over the ten seeds, and give \pCi\% bootstrap intervals over seeds.
The map-aided tracker was developed after the dominant-path estimator,
on the same tuning and development seeds, and was run once on the
held-out seeds with unchanged parameters.

\subsection{What Limits the Bound}
Fig.~\ref{fig:bw} shows the median bounds versus bandwidth. With LoS to
both O-RUs, the LoS-only PEB is below \pDecThr\,cm in
\pDecLosFourHundredLos{} of the epochs at \pBwC\,MHz and still in
\pDecLosHundredLos{} at \pBwA\,MHz. Relaxing one limit at a time at
\pBwB\,MHz scales the median LoS-only PEB (\pLimLosBase\,cm) by
\pLimLosBw{} for twice the bandwidth, \pLimLosSnr{} for ten times the
SNR, \pLimLosSync{} for perfect synchronization, and \pLimLosCal{} for
perfect calibration. On these short links, the delay and angle
measurements are precise enough that the element phase errors, which
bias the angles, dominate. Without hardware errors, the LoS-only PEB is
below \pDecThr\,cm in \pHwLosIdeal{} of the epochs; with
\pSyncWorst\,ns and \pPhiWorst\,deg errors, in \pHwLosWorst. The
map-aided PEB is far less sensitive (\pHwMapIdeal{} and \pHwMapWorst),
because reflections from known surfaces add independent geometric
information.



\subsection{Blockage and the Digital Twin}
In the biased model, the median LoS-only PEB rises by a factor of
\pRatioLosB{} during blockage ($p=\pRatioPLosB$), and only
\pDecLosFourHundredBlk{} of the blocked epochs remain below \pDecThr\,cm.
If the attenuated LoS kept its geometry, the factor would be only
\pRatioLosA, which shows how optimistic it is to apply model~B's power
loss without its geometric consequence. Map-aided information lowers
the PEB during blockage to \pMapToLosBlk{} times the LoS-only value
($p=\pMapToLosP$). Along the street, the
LoS-only PEB varies with the geometry of the two O-RUs from about a
centimeter to over a decimeter, while the map-aided PEB stays mostly in
the millimeter range. A low map-aided PEB indicates that the
information is present in the signal; whether an estimator can extract
it is a separate question.



\subsection{Practical Estimators and the Bound}
At \pBwC\,MHz, the dominant-path estimator reaches a median error of
\pEstMedFourHundred\,cm (\pEstLosFourHundred\,cm with LoS to both O-RUs,
\pEstBlkFourHundred\,cm during blockage); \pEstDecFourHundred{} of its
errors are below \pDecThr\,cm, and its 90th percentile is
\pEstPninetyFourHundred\,m. At \pBwA\,MHz, the
median grows to \pEstMedHundred\,cm.

To compare the estimator with the bound on equal terms, we ran
\pValNumDraws{} independent noise and hardware draws through the
single-epoch estimator (without EKF) at \pValNumGeom{} fixed geometries
and decomposed its error as
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RMSE}^2=\|\mathbb E[\hat{\mathbf p}]-\mathbf p\|^2
+\mathbb E\bigl[\|\hat{\mathbf p}-\mathbb E[\hat{\mathbf p}]\|^2\bigr],
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
i.e., squared bias plus squared centered RMS (Table~\ref{tab:val}). The
hardware errors are redrawn in every draw, so their contribution appears
in the centered RMS, whereas the bias contains only deterministic errors
such as unresolved multipath and the detoured LoS; within a run, a
hardware realization is fixed and acts as a bias. The squared bias accounts for most of the mean squared error in five of the
six geometries at \pBwC\,MHz (\pValBiasMseFour{} of it); in the
remaining one (lamppost, both LoS, $x=-20$\,m), the squared bias is
\pValBiasMseFourOther{} of it and the random spread is larger. The bias is caused by interference of unresolved multipath, and
in one geometry by a front/back mirror solution. The centered RMS is
geometry dependent, between \pValCenToPebFour{} times the PEB at
\pBwC\,MHz: close to the bound at some geometries and an order of
magnitude above it at others. In blocked geometries it falls below the
PEB, which is possible because the estimator is biased: it uses the
detoured LoS precisely but in the wrong place. Over the held-out runs, front/back
mirror solutions remain in \pMirrorShareEpochs{} of the epochs and
dominate the squared error (RMS \pMirrorARmse\,m), while reflected
dominant paths set the 90th percentile. A variant that used reflections
as virtual anchors did not reduce the tail on the development seeds
(90th percentile \pDevBPninety{} vs.\ \pDevAPninety\,m) and was not
pursued.

The map-aided tracker reaches a median error of \pTrkMed\,cm (\pTrkBlkMed\,cm
with at least one O-RU blocked) and a 90th percentile of
\pTrkPninety\,m, significantly better than the dominant-path estimator
(all $p=\pTrkVsAPAll$). Its largest errors
occur in the \SI{1}{s} after a UE re-enters the periodic street section,
a simulation artifact in which the track keeps the pre-jump position
until it re-initializes; these windows contain \pTrkWrapShare{} of the
epochs (not excluded).

\subsection{Positioning Errors Around Blockage Onsets}
Table~\ref{tab:onset} shows the estimator error around the
\pOnsetNumEvents{} blockage events of the reference cell, defined per UE
as the cell with the larger unblocked received power; an event is an
interval with LoS loss of at least \SI{10}{dB}, with gaps below
\SI{0.5}{s} merged; each epoch is counted in one window, with priority
to the event, and the first \SI{2}{s} of each run are excluded. In the second before onset, a window relevant to proactive
handover, the median error and the share of errors above
\SI{0.25}{m} are about as elsewhere, but the large errors become larger:
the 90th percentile rises from \pOnsetElsePninety{} to
\pOnsetPreHalfPninety\,m. During the event, most of the distribution
deteriorates, with the median growing to \pOnsetDuringMed\,cm. Errors thus
worsen in the time windows relevant to proactive handover: before a
blockage, the extreme errors grow; during it, the bulk of the errors
does, which the overall median hides. For the map-aided tracker, the
90th percentile does not rise before onset (\pTrkOnsetPreHalfPninety{}
vs.\ \pTrkOnsetElsePninety\,m elsewhere), and its median during the
event is \pTrkOnsetDuringMed\,cm. The windows refer to the blockage
events of a reference cell, not to the decision times of a particular
planner.





\subsection{Implications for Handover}
Table~\ref{tab:closing} feeds positions into the planner, with perfect blocker tracks so that the user position is
the only source of error. With exact positions, the planner has a lower
outage than A5 from \pMarginFifteen\,dB margin upward (significantly at
15 and \SI{25}{dB} and at the reference). As a sensitivity test, we feed
synthetic errors $\mathbf e_t\sim\mathcal N\bigl(\mathbf 0,\mathbf
J_{\mathbf p}(t)^{-1}\bigr)$, drawn independently per epoch from the
single-epoch bound of the respective model, which is anisotropic
(median ratio of principal standard deviations \pAniso). Errors at the
map-aided bound preserve a lower mean outage than A5 at most tested
margins, with significant improvements at 15 and \SI{25}{dB}; errors at
the LoS-only bound, which grow during blockage, make it worse than A5 at
every margin. With the positions of the dominant-path estimator, the
planner is significantly worse than A5 at every margin, also with ideal
hardware; with those of the map-aided tracker, it is not significantly
different from A5 at any margin from \SI{15}{dB} up ($p\ge\pTrkMinP$),
although at \SI{25}{dB} its bootstrap interval lies above zero; at
\SI{10}{dB} it is still significantly worse (\pCloseTrkTen~s/UE-min,
$p=\pCloseTrkTenP$), as at all lower margins ($p\le\pTrkLowMaxP$). Capping each error of the EKF output at the
pooled 90th percentile (\pCapPninety\,m) or replacing its mirror
solutions by the single-epoch hypothesis closest to the true position
(an oracle diagnostic, not refiltered) reduces the loss but leaves it
significant, so the largest errors are not its only
cause. Synthetic bound-level errors are an optimistic model; they show what
positioning would have to achieve, not that a practical estimator
achieves it. In the \pDualBlockShare{} of epochs in which both O-RUs are
blocked, the LoS-only bound is singular; these epochs are treated as
having no fix (\SI{100}{m} standard deviation). Holding the last valid
position instead keeps the LoS-only row above A5 at every margin but
removes its significance, so that row depends mainly on how missing
fixes are handled. The synthetic errors are independent
across epochs, whereas hardware errors persist over a run; the effect of
temporal correlation on the planner is not evaluated here. The absolute gains at stake are small: at \SI{15}{dB} margin, A5 has an
outage of \pAfiveFifteen~s/UE-min, and the relative change is
\mbox{\pRelPerfFifteen} with exact positions and \mbox{\pRelMapFifteen} with
bound-level map-aided positions; at the reference, A5 has
\pAfiveRef~s/UE-min, and the changes are \mbox{\pRelPerfRef} and
\mbox{\pRelMapRef}, respectively; the application-level impact is not evaluated here.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Discussion and Limitations}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
For blockage-aware handover, the relevant accuracy is the error around
blockage onsets, not the overall median. The gap between the bound and
the dominant-path estimator is mainly systematic multipath error, and a
simple virtual-anchor variant did not close it; the map-aided tracker,
which resolves several paths and associates them probabilistically with
known surfaces, does extract much of the information that the map-aided
bound indicates. With its positions, proactive handover is no longer
significantly worse than A5 at margins from \SI{15}{dB} up, but it does not reach the small gain that exact positions
provide; whether this remaining gap stems from the tracker's residual
errors, including those after the UE jumps, is not resolved here. The study has
limitations: a single street canyon with two O-RUs; a tracker added
after the first evaluation and run with an exact map; specular paths of
order at most \pMaxDepth{} and no diffuse scattering; a perfectly known
map in the map-aided bound; the biased model of a blocked LoS as our
modeling choice; bodies as opaque knife-edge screens, without window
transmission, vehicle-body reflections, or self-blockage by the user; single-epoch bounds without the temporal information
of tracking; and planar arrays of isotropic elements, whose front/back
ambiguity a back plane would remove
\cite{dong2026deployable}.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
Under the tested configurations, the single-epoch bound is limited by
array calibration rather than bandwidth; blockage substantially degrades
the LoS-only bound, while known reflected paths preserve a much lower
map-aided bound. A dominant-path estimator shows substantial systematic
multipath errors that worsen around blockage, and its positions make
proactive handover worse than reactive handover. A map-aided multipath
tracker reduces the median error to a few centimeters and keeps it low
around blockage onsets, so that proactive handover is no longer
significantly worse than the 3GPP event at margins of \SI{15}{dB} and
above; reaching the small gain that exact positions
provide remains open.
