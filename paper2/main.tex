\title{Infrastructure-Based mmWave Positioning\\
for Blockage-Aware Handover:\\
Bounds and Practical Limits}

\begin{abstract}
Blockage-aware proactive handover in millimeter-wave (mmWave) networks
predicts when a body will cross the line of sight (LoS) between a radio
unit and a user, and is therefore only as good as the network's
knowledge of the user position. This paper studies whether the radio
units that serve a user can localize it accurately enough from its
uplink sounding reference signals. We derive single-epoch position error
bounds that include synchronization and array calibration errors as
Bayesian priors, contrast LoS-only information with map-aided
information from a digital twin, and evaluate a practical estimator
with an extended Kalman filter. In ray-traced experiments in a street
canyon, reported on held-out scenarios, the bound is limited by array
calibration rather than bandwidth and degrades during blockage unless
reflected paths are exploited through the digital twin. The practical
estimator is close to the bound in its random error but suffers from
systematic multipath errors, which grow by an order of magnitude during
blockage events. Fed into a blockage-aware handover planner, its
positions do not let foresight outperform 3GPP event-triggered handover,
whereas synthetic errors at the level of the map-aided bound do.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
Millimeter-wave (mmWave) bands provide the bandwidth that 5G and 6G
networks need for high data rates, but their links are fragile: a
pedestrian or vehicle crossing the line of sight (LoS) attenuates the
signal by tens of decibels within milliseconds
\cite{rappaport2013mmwave,maccartney2017blockage}. Reactive mobility
procedures, which hand over after measurements have degraded, cannot
avoid such outages. Integrated sensing and communication (ISAC) and
other sensing-aided approaches therefore aim to predict blockage and
hand the user over in advance
\cite{liu2022isac,charan2021vision,li2025lookbefore}.

Predicting a blockage means predicting whether a body will cross the
line between a radio unit and the user. The prediction can thus be no
better than the network's knowledge of where the user is. In a companion
study \cite{natanzi2027companion}, we found that a blockage-aware
handover planner outperforms the service-aware 3GPP A5 event only if
the user position is known with an error of about \pBreakEven\,cm (per
horizontal axis), and that a meter-level error removes the benefit
entirely. Positioning accuracy is therefore not a side issue but a
prerequisite of sensing-aided mobility.

Can the network meet this requirement with the infrastructure it
already has? 5G NR supports uplink positioning from sounding reference
signals (SRS) \cite{3gpp38855}, and recent street-scale studies with
several base stations report errors below a meter
\cite{dong2026deployable}. Position error bounds for mmWave systems
suggest that much better accuracy is possible in principle
\cite{shahmansoori2018position,abushaban2018bounds}, but they usually
assume ideal hardware and static scenes. It remains unclear which effect
limits decimeter accuracy in practice, how blockage changes it, how
close practical estimators come, and whether their errors are small
enough at the moments when a handover decision is made.

This paper addresses these questions in a street canyon served by two
O-RAN radio units (O-RUs). Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item A positioning analysis tailored to blockage-aware handover: a
single-epoch equivalent Fisher information analysis with
synchronization and array calibration errors as Bayesian priors, two
models of a blocked LoS, and map-aided information from reflected paths
with known geometry.
\item A practical uplink estimator with an extended Kalman filter (EKF),
validated against the bound at fixed geometries, which separates its
random error from systematic multipath errors.
\item An analysis of positioning errors around blockage onsets and a
closing experiment that feeds estimated and synthetic bound-level
positions into a blockage-aware handover planner.
\item Findings on held-out scenarios: calibration rather than bandwidth
limits the bound; blockage degrades it unless the digital twin is used;
and practical errors are largest exactly when handover decisions are
made.
\end{itemize}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Related Work}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\textit{Bounds for mmWave positioning.} Fisher information analyses
show that the angle and delay resolution of mmWave arrays support
sub-decimeter accuracy \cite{shahmansoori2018position,abushaban2018bounds},
building on the equivalent Fisher information framework for wideband
localization \cite{shen2010fundamental}. Multipath components with known
geometry carry position information as virtual anchors
\cite{leitinger2015evaluation,witrisal2016high}. We use these tools, add
hardware errors as priors, and evaluate them under traffic-driven
blockage.

\textit{Uplink SRS positioning.} 3GPP specifies uplink time- and
angle-based positioning \cite{3gpp38855}. Dong \emph{et al.}
\cite{dong2026deployable} combine LoS/NLoS gating, dominant-path angle
and delay estimation, and multi-base-station EKF fusion, reach sub-meter
errors in a ray-traced campus, and point to reflections as virtual
anchors for future work. Our estimator follows a similar architecture;
our question is whether its accuracy suffices for a specific downstream
use.

\textit{Sensing-aided handover.} Vision-, radar-, and ISAC-aided
handover predicts blockage or user motion
\cite{charan2021vision,li2025lookbefore}. Our companion study
\cite{natanzi2027companion} separated the value of foresight from what
sensing realizes and identified the user position as the binding
requirement; this paper examines whether the infrastructure can meet it.

\section{System Model and Problem Formulation}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Signal Model}
A single-antenna user equipment (UE) at horizontal position $\mathbf p$
transmits an uplink SRS that is received by a set $\mathcal C$ of O-RUs,
each with an $M$-element array. At O-RU $c$, subcarrier frequency
$f_n$, and element $m$, the received sample is
\begin{equation}
y_{c,n,m}=\sum_{\ell}\beta_{c,\ell}\,
e^{-j2\pi f_n(\tau_{c,\ell}+\delta_c+b)}\,
e^{j\psi_{c,m}}\,s_m(\mathbf u_{c,\ell})+w_{c,n,m},
\label{eq:signal}
\end{equation}
where path $\ell$ has complex gain $\beta_{c,\ell}$ (including blockage
loss), delay $\tau_{c,\ell}$, and arrival direction
$\mathbf u_{c,\ell}$ with steering response $s_m$; $\delta_c$ is the time
offset of O-RU $c$, $b$ the UE clock bias, $\psi_{c,m}$ the calibration
phase error of element $m$, and $w_{c,n,m}$ thermal noise. Hardware
errors are random with $\delta_c\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{sync}}^2)$
and $\psi_{c,m}\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_\phi^2)$.

\subsection{Geometry and Blockage}
The LoS delay and angles are functions of $\mathbf p$ and the O-RU
position $\mathbf p_c$, e.g., $\tau_{c,0}=\|\mathbf p-\mathbf p_c\|/c_0$.
A specular reflection on a known surface is an LoS path from the virtual
anchor $\tilde{\mathbf p}_{c,\ell}$, the image of $\mathbf p_c$ in that
surface, so that $\tau_{c,\ell}=\|\mathbf p-\tilde{\mathbf p}_{c,\ell}\|/c_0$
\cite{leitinger2015evaluation}. Moving bodies attenuate every path
segment according to 3GPP blockage model~B \cite{3gpp38901}. An O-RU is
\emph{blocked} for a UE at an epoch when the model-B loss of its LoS path
is at least \pBlockedDb\,dB. Model~B reduces the power of a blocked LoS
but keeps its geometry, whereas the field that actually arrives
diffracts around the blocker. We therefore consider two models of a
blocked LoS: \emph{kept}, in which its delay and angles still follow
$\mathbf p$ (optimistic), and \emph{biased}, in which they carry an
unknown bias. In the biased model, the bound treats them as free
parameters, and the signal fed to the estimator routes the blocked LoS
over or around its dominant blocker, so that the estimator also sees a
biased path.

\subsection{Positioning Requirement of Blockage-Aware Handover}
The handover planner of \cite{natanzi2027companion} receives tracks of
the blockers and the reported UE position every \pEpoch\,s, predicts the
signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of each cell by applying model~B on the line
between each O-RU and the reported position, and chooses the serving
cell sequence that minimizes the predicted outage, accounting for the
interruption of every handover. Its performance is measured by the
outage time at a service rate, compared with the 3GPP A5 event. With
perfect blocker tracks and a white Gaussian UE position error, the
planner beats A5 only up to a per-axis standard deviation of about
\pBreakEven\,cm, i.e., an RMS distance error of about \pBreakEvenRms\,cm or a
median distance error of about \pBreakEvenMedDist\,cm. The positioning problem is
thus to estimate $\mathbf p$ at every decision epoch such that the
resulting handover outage stays below that of A5; we examine (i) what
limits the bound, (ii) how blockage and map knowledge change it, (iii)
how a practical estimator compares, and (iv) whether its positions
suffice for the planner.

\section{Position Error Bound and Estimator}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Single-Epoch Position Error Bound}
Collect the channel parameters of all O-RUs at one epoch in
$\boldsymbol\eta$ (delays, angles, complex gains) and the hardware and
timing errors in $\boldsymbol\nu=[\{\delta_c\},b,\{\psi_{c,m}\}]$. The
Fisher information of $\boldsymbol\eta$ follows from \eqref{eq:signal}
by the Slepian--Bangs formula; we compute it exactly by factorizing its
frequency and antenna sums. With $\mathbf T=\partial\boldsymbol\eta/\partial\mathbf p$,
the equivalent Fisher information of the position is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf J_{\mathbf p}=\mathbf T^{\mathsf T}\mathbf J_{\boldsymbol\eta}\mathbf T
-\mathbf T^{\mathsf T}\mathbf J_{\boldsymbol\eta\boldsymbol\nu}
\bigl(\mathbf J_{\boldsymbol\nu\boldsymbol\nu}+\boldsymbol\Lambda\bigr)^{-1}
\mathbf J_{\boldsymbol\nu\boldsymbol\eta}\mathbf T,
\label{eq:efim}
\end{equation}
after the nuisance parameters have been removed by Schur complements:
the complex gains of all paths; in the \emph{LoS-only} bound, the delays
and angles of all reflected paths; in the biased model, the delay and
angles of a blocked LoS; and the timing and hardware errors in
$\boldsymbol\nu$. $\boldsymbol\Lambda$ holds the Gaussian priors of the
O-RU time offsets ($\sigma_{\mathrm{sync}}$) and of the element phases
($\sigma_\phi$); the UE clock bias has no prior (time difference of
arrival, TDoA). In the \emph{map-aided} bound, the reflected paths are
not nuisance parameters: their geometry is known from the digital twin,
and their entries of $\mathbf T$ follow analytically from the virtual
anchors, validated by re-tracing at $\pm\pFdStep$\,cm. The position
error bound is $\mathrm{PEB}=\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf J_{\mathbf p}^{-1})}$.
It bounds the root-mean-square (RMS) error of unbiased single-epoch
estimates for a given geometry; it is neither a median error nor a
guarantee that an estimator attains it, and it excludes the temporal
information that a tracking filter accumulates.

\subsection{Practical Estimator}
Per O-RU and epoch, a zero-padded two-dimensional DFT over the array
selects the strongest beam; a Hann-windowed delay transform of the
beamformed signal gives the delay of the dominant path, refined by
parabolic interpolation and followed by an angle refinement at that
delay. A peak-to-second-peak test flags reflected dominant paths.
Because a planar array of isotropic elements cannot distinguish the two
sides of its plane, each angle yields two direction hypotheses; the
estimator keeps the joint hypothesis that is consistent across O-RUs,
lies in the walkable area of the digital-twin map, and passes the gate
of a constant-velocity EKF, which fuses weighted least-squares fixes
over time.

\section{Experiments and Results}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Setup}
\textit{Scenario.} We use the street canyon of
\cite{natanzi2027companion}: two O-RUs with \pArray{} half-wavelength
arrays at \pCarrier\,GHz (one on a lamppost or a facade on one side, one
on a lamppost on the opposite sidewalk), pedestrian UEs at
\pUeHeight\,m, and traffic of cars, buses, trucks, and pedestrians. Paths
are traced with Sionna RT \cite{hoydis2023sionnart,sionna} (specular,
order up to \pMaxDepth, at most \pMaxPaths{} paths per link).

\textit{Signals and hardware.} The UE transmits one SRS symbol per
\pEpoch-s epoch at \pTxPower\,dBm; the O-RU noise figure is
\pNoiseFig\,dB. Numerology~\pNumerology{} (\pScs\,kHz) with \pBwA,
\pBwB, and \pBwC\,MHz carriers uses \pNscA, \pNscB, and \pNscC{} active
subcarriers \cite{3gpp38101_2}. We sweep
$\sigma_{\mathrm{sync}}\in\{\pSyncList\}$\,ns (drawn once per run, a bias
that the EKF cannot average out) and $\sigma_\phi\in\{\pPhiList\}$\,deg
(fixed per O-RU and run). The main configuration uses TDoA, \pBwC\,MHz,
$\sigma_{\mathrm{sync}}=\pSyncMain$\,ns, $\sigma_\phi=\pPhiMain$\,deg, and
the biased blocked-LoS model.

\textit{Protocol.} Estimator parameters were tuned on seeds
\pSeedsTune{} and methods fixed on development seeds \pSeedsDev. An
earlier held-out set was consumed before the front/back disambiguation
was added, so all results come from a second, never-used set of ten
seeds (\pSeedsHeld), each simulated for two mounts and two traffic
densities. The seed is the statistical unit: we average its runs,
compare conditions with the exact two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test
over the ten seeds, and give \pCi\% bootstrap intervals over seeds.

\subsection{What Limits the Bound}
Fig.~\ref{fig:bw} shows the median bounds versus bandwidth. With LoS to
both O-RUs, the LoS-only PEB is below \pDecThr\,cm in
\pDecLosFourHundredLos{} of the epochs at \pBwC\,MHz and still in
\pDecLosHundredLos{} at \pBwA\,MHz. Relaxing one limit at a time at
\pBwB\,MHz scales the median LoS-only PEB (\pLimLosBase\,cm) by
\pLimLosBw{} for twice the bandwidth, \pLimLosSnr{} for ten times the
SNR, \pLimLosSync{} for perfect synchronization, and \pLimLosCal{} for
perfect calibration. On these short links, the delay and angle
measurements are precise enough that the element phase errors, which
bias the angles, dominate. Without hardware errors, the LoS-only PEB is
below \pDecThr\,cm in \pHwLosIdeal{} of the epochs; with
\pSyncWorst\,ns and \pPhiWorst\,deg errors, in \pHwLosWorst. The
map-aided PEB is far less sensitive (\pHwMapIdeal{} and \pHwMapWorst),
because reflections from known surfaces add independent geometric
information.

\subsection{Blockage and the Digital Twin}
In the biased model, the median LoS-only PEB rises by a factor of
\pRatioLosB{} during blockage ($p=\pRatioPLosB$), and only
\pDecLosFourHundredBlk{} of the blocked epochs remain below \pDecThr\,cm.
If the attenuated LoS kept its geometry, the factor would be only
\pRatioLosA, which shows how optimistic it is to apply model~B's power
loss without its geometric consequence. Map-aided information lowers
the PEB during blockage to \pMapToLosBlk{} times the LoS-only value
($p=\pMapToLosP$). Along the street (Fig.~\ref{fig:sidewalk}), the
LoS-only PEB varies with the geometry of the two O-RUs from about a
centimeter to over a decimeter, while the map-aided PEB stays mostly in
the millimeter range. A low map-aided PEB indicates that the
information is present in the signal; whether an estimator can extract
it is a separate question.

\subsection{Practical Estimator and the Bound}
At \pBwC\,MHz, the estimator reaches a median error of
\pEstMedFourHundred\,cm (\pEstLosFourHundred\,cm with LoS to both O-RUs,
\pEstBlkFourHundred\,cm during blockage); \pEstDecFourHundred{} of its
errors are below \pDecThr\,cm, and its 90th percentile is
\pEstPninetyFourHundred\,m (Fig.~\ref{fig:cdf}). At \pBwA\,MHz, the
median grows to \pEstMedHundred\,cm.

To compare the estimator with the bound on equal terms, we ran
\pValNumDraws{} independent noise and hardware draws through the
single-epoch estimator (without EKF) at \pValNumGeom{} fixed geometries.
With LoS to both O-RUs, the random spread of the estimates around their
mean is \pValSpreadToPeb{} times the PEB, i.e., close to the bound, but
the mean itself is offset from the true position by
\pValBiasLos\,m, caused by interference of unresolved multipath; the
largest offset is a front/back mirror solution. The RMS error is
therefore \pValRmseToPeb{} times the PEB. In blocked geometries, the
offset is \pValBiasBlk\,m. The gap to the bound is thus a systematic
error, not a lack of precision. Over the held-out runs, front/back
mirror solutions remain in \pMirrorShareEpochs{} of the epochs and
dominate the squared error (RMS \pMirrorARmse\,m), while reflected
dominant paths set the 90th percentile. A variant that used reflections
as virtual anchors did not reduce the tail on the development seeds
(90th percentile \pDevBPninety{} vs.\ \pDevAPninety\,m) and was not
pursued.

\subsection{Errors When Handover Decisions Are Made}
Table~\ref{tab:onset} shows the estimator error around the blockage
events of the serving cell. In the second before onset, when a
blockage-aware planner must decide, the median error is as small as
elsewhere, but the 90th percentile is almost three times larger
(\pOnsetPreHalfPninety{} vs.\ \pOnsetElsePninety\,m), and about half of
the errors exceed \SI{0.1}{m}. During the event, the median grows by an
order of magnitude to \pOnsetDuringMed\,cm. The error is therefore
largest exactly when the planner needs the position, which the overall
median hides.

\subsection{Implications for Handover}
Fig.~\ref{fig:closing} and Table~\ref{tab:closing} feed positions into
the planner, with perfect blocker tracks so that the user position is
the only source of error. With exact positions, the planner has a lower
outage than A5 from \pMarginFifteen\,dB margin upward (significantly at
15 and \SI{25}{dB} and at the reference). As a sensitivity test, we feed
synthetic errors drawn independently per epoch from the full inverse of
the single-epoch $\mathbf J_{\mathbf p}$, which is anisotropic (median
ratio of principal standard deviations \pAniso). Errors at the
map-aided bound keep the planner at or below A5 (significantly below at
15 and \SI{25}{dB}); errors at the LoS-only bound, which grow during
blockage, make it worse than A5 at every margin. With the estimator's
positions, the planner is significantly worse than A5 at every margin,
also with ideal hardware. Capping the errors at their 90th percentile or
removing the mirror solutions with the true position reduces the loss
but leaves it significant: removing the largest errors is not enough,
because errors of a few decimeters around blockage onsets already
mislead the planner. Synthetic bound-level errors are an optimistic
model; they show what positioning would have to achieve, not that a
practical estimator achieves it.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Discussion and Limitations}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
For blockage-aware handover, the relevant accuracy is the error around
blockage onsets, not the overall median, and the gap between the bound
and our estimator is systematic multipath error rather than noise. The
information to close this gap is present in the reflected paths, as the
map-aided bound shows, but our simple virtual-anchor variant did not
extract it; map-aided estimators that reliably associate reflections
with known surfaces \cite{witrisal2016high,dong2026deployable} and
better array calibration are the main levers. The study has
limitations: a single street canyon with two O-RUs; specular paths of
order at most \pMaxDepth{} and no diffuse scattering; a perfectly known
map in the map-aided bound; the biased model of a blocked LoS as our
modelling choice; single-epoch bounds without the temporal information
of tracking; and planar arrays of isotropic elements, whose front/back
ambiguity a physical back plane would remove in hardware
\cite{dong2026deployable}.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
We examined whether the mmWave radio units that serve a user can
localize it accurately enough for blockage-aware handover. The
single-epoch bound shows that decimeter accuracy is limited by array
calibration rather than bandwidth and is lost during blockage unless
reflected paths are exploited through the digital twin. A practical
estimator is close to the bound in its random error but suffers from
systematic multipath errors that peak around blockage onsets, exactly
when handover decisions are made, and its positions do not let
foresight outperform reactive handover. Map-aided estimation that turns
reflections into reliable position information is therefore the next
step toward ISAC-assisted mobility.
